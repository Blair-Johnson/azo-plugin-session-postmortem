# Native semantic-investigation trials

This round used the actual installed trace tools at commit `efd202a`, not CLI substitutes or direct transcript reads. Two saved GLIDR research sessions supplied 11,602 indexed events. An independent setter prepared two questions and a separate answer key; blind investigators used separate workspaces, and an independent grader checked the frozen attempts. A third investigation began without a directed question and passed its authored map to a fresh investigator without sharing its report or conversation. This is a functional investigation trial, not a randomized comparison against an earlier toolset. Call counts below are investigator-reported rather than independently recounted.

## What investigators reconstructed

The cross-session investigation used 30 native calls without errors. It connected changes in rule extraction and evaluation to a manuscript snapshot, read the saved numerical tables, and found that updated results and older explanatory prose disagreed. It distinguished strong performance from eight-rule ensembles from claims the records did not establish: isolated expressivity gains, soft-model prediction fidelity, and universal superiority over larger systems. Its nine map entries and six relations survived reopening. The independent grade was 10/10 after checking pivotal claims against the saved source.

The bottleneck investigation used 33 native calls, including three recovered read failures. It reconstructed why repeated ranking, Prolog execution, and soft inference were bottlenecks at different times: they involved different implementations, scales, and measured scopes. It followed a JIT-hoisting recommendation through the user's demand for proof to the actual-path test: 66.583 versus 66.490 seconds, about 1.0014×. The investigator did not multiply primitive speedups into an unsupported end-to-end claim, and distinguished the completed Family test from unresolved Freebase profiling. The independent grade was 9.8/10: the answer omitted the JAX/fork warning and duplicated databases that helped explain rejection of the process-pool alternative. The grader found no substantive wrong assertion among the checked claims.

Open-ended discovery took 20 native calls, including one rejected read. Starting from a human-turn timeline and a structural sample, the investigator found repeated tension between requested narrow changes and the behavior actually verified. It linked an initially passing graph-free audit to a later serialized-rule mismatch, a requested restart, and bounded recovery. It also retained contrary evidence rather than turning the session into a failure narrative. Six episodes and a pinned continuation note recorded evidence, hypotheses, unreviewed areas, and next probes. A fresh investigator, given only the workspace, continued in 21 native calls. It read the cards and links, then resolved three gaps: the graph-free comparison shared the changed extraction helper while the separate historical check covered only the dataset module, the symmetric metric certificate was repaired with a float tolerance, and raw timings supported withdrawal of the JIT claim. It also qualified the earlier narrative: some limited test scope was explicitly user-requested, and the initial replay requirement was ambiguous. The parent independently expanded the new audit episode and checked the recorded scripts and mismatch output. This is evidence of useful continuation and revision, not just persistence of notes.

## Tool-design lessons

The useful workflow was structural selection, bounded reading, source searches within chosen regions, and investigator-authored episode cards. The map was not a generated summary endpoint: its conclusions, uncertainty, and relations came from the investigator. Reports stayed in ordinary files. Reopening and expanding a card's anchors worked, and query/search selections could be retained for later exploration.

The prior reload had exposed a real schema bug that unit tests missed: the model could not populate map entries. The explicit nested schemas fixed that in live use. The subsequent trials found smaller friction. Guessing line bounds caused repeated failures; a cursor continuation rejected explicitly supplied expansion defaults; and one interface treated optional line bounds as mandatory. Targeted refinements preserve strict ranges and immutable cursor plans while exposing whole-field selectors and giving actionable recovery instructions. The continuation also exposed a silent negative: searching an opaque saved buffer returned zero hits before a later read revealed that its content was unavailable. Search now returns bounded notices and a complete count of known unsearchable sources within the selected scope, retaining that information across pages. It does not recover missing content or certify historical completeness. The combined refinements passed all 54 tests in `rx r5`; they have not yet been rerun through a reloaded model interface.

Broad lexical searches were often a poor first move. One cross-session regex produced 4,446 matches, dominated by code and documentation; structural human-turn queries were more useful for orientation. Such a timeline is still biased toward expressed intent and visible correction, so it must be checked against tool outputs and contrary episodes. Original output truncation is separate from tool pagination and remains a limit. Exact-collapse occurrence IDs and response metadata were noisy; these observations do not yet justify another high-level tool or an automatic summarizer.

## Scope and artifacts

The indexed event count is not the number of events read, and map membership is not review coverage. These trials do not establish broad task success rates, exhaustive discovery, or a causal improvement from the toolkit.
